Our micromouse drive motor

Bashar Ghosheh · Amro Qutna · Tareq Ahmad · Ahmad AlSheikh · Dr. Wasel Ghanem

A micromouse wins by holding the speed it is told, on both wheels, without slipping. Here we model the N20 gear motor we ordered, choose a spec for its speed loop, design a PI controller, and generate the Arduino code.

1 · The plant and where its numbers come from

τ·y′ + y = K·u

The plant is one drive wheel: input u = PWM duty (%), output y = wheel speed from the encoder (rpm). A DC gear motor driving the robot's inertia is first order, like the sensors of Module 1.

Our robot is still being built, so these numbers come from the motor's datasheet, as the sheet allows. When the robot is assembled, section 5 replaces them with a measured step test.

    2 · The spec and why it matters for a micromouse

    0.6 ≤ ζ ≤ 0.8

    The practical range from the lecture. Less damping overshoots the speed: the wheels slip, the encoder count no longer matches the distance, and the mouse loses track of where it is in the maze.

    PWM inside 0–100%

    The driver cannot give more than the battery. If the PID asks for more, the command clips, the response no longer matches the design, and the integral winds up.

    Zero steady-state error

    Two motors are never identical. With P alone each wheel settles at a different speed and the mouse curves into a wall. The integral term makes both wheels reach the same speed.

    Cruise at 300 rpm

    The step we design for: about 0.5 m/s with our wheels, a sensible exploring speed for a 18 cm maze cell.

    3 · Design: the gains and the simulated response

    Ki = ωn²τ/K · Kp = (2ζωnτ − 1)/K

    Pick the damping ζ and the speed ωn of the closed loop. The PI gains follow directly (the same design as P4's reference), and the loop is simulated as a digital PID with the real PWM limits and anti-windup.

    Predict, then check

    Later we add a heavier battery, so the robot's mass doubles. τ grows. If we keep the same gains, does the overshoot go up or down?

    5 · Measure it yourself, when the robot is built

    1. Lift the robot so the wheel spins freely, or let it drive straight on the floor.
    2. Apply a PWM step (for example 0 → 50%) and log time_ms, rpm from the encoder every 2–5 ms for about 0.3 s.
